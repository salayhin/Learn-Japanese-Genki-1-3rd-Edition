Reference · Course plan

The Genki I Path

Nine weeks, five weekdays each, fifty minutes a day. From no Japanese at all to reading both syllabaries fluently and holding the first five lessons of Genki I. No prior knowledge assumed, and nothing to do at weekends.

Who this is for

Someone starting from zero — no kana, no vocabulary, no grammar — who has about 45–50 minutes on weekdays and nothing at weekends, and who wants reading as the primary outcome rather than a side effect. It assumes you own the Genki I textbook and workbook and are working without a teacher, so every exercise here is self-scoring.

The plan is 45 sessions: nine weeks, five days per week, one session a day. It uses no calendar dates, so it starts whenever you start.

What nine weeks actually buys — read this before starting

45 sessions of 50 minutes is about 37 hours. Genki I is a full university semester, conventionally 90–120 hours of study. So this plan does not cover all twelve lessons, and any plan that claims to at this pace is lying to you.

What it does cover, properly: the entire writing system except kanji, then Genki Lessons 1–5. That is roughly 43 kanji, 200 words, and the grammar to talk about what you do, what you did, and what things are like — the core of JLPT N5. Lessons 6–12 are mapped at the bottom for the months after. Covering five lessons you retain beats covering twelve you don't.

What you need

Two books, both by Banno, Ikeda, Ohno, Shinagawa and Tokashiki, published by The Japan Times:

  • GENKI I: An Integrated Course in Elementary Japanese, 3rd edition (ISBN 978-4-7890-1730-5)
  • GENKI I Workbook, 3rd edition (ISBN 978-4-7890-1731-2)

Page references below are the numbers printed on the page, so they work in any copy — print or official ebook. Workbook references are marked workbook; everything else is the textbook.

Audio is free from the publisher's OTO Navi app (iOS / Android) — search the textbook's ISBN inside the app. Install it before day 1; the reading protocol below does not work without it. The publisher also runs a free Self-Study Room with extra materials.

The textbook has two halves numbered continuously: 会話・文法編 (Conversation & Grammar, pp. 30–293) and 読み書き編 (Reading & Writing, from p. 296). That second half is the one most self-learners skip, and it is the one this plan is built around. The workbook mirrors the split, with one worksheet per grammar point, numbered to match the textbook's own grammar numbering.

Phase 0 · The Writing System

Weeks 1–3 · 13 sessions

Everything downstream is gated on this. Every vocabulary list, every grammar drill, every passage is written in kana — so time spent here is not time away from Japanese, it is the only thing that makes the rest possible.

There are 104 readings per script: 46 basic characters, 25 voiced, 33 contracted. That sounds enormous and isn't, because the last 58 are three rules applied to the first 46. Two and a half weeks is enough if the order is right.

Two principles behind the ordering

Hiragana before katakana. Hiragana is the default script — grammar, particles, verb endings, anything without a kanji. It also pays forward: about twenty katakana come from the same source kanji as a hiragana, so they come almost free once hiragana is solid. Learning katakana first throws that away.

Look-alikes last, within each script. Telling シ from ツ is discrimination — it sharpens a distinction between two things you already hold. Taught before you know the characters, it just produces shapes that all blur together. So each script ends with a consolidation day and a traps day, never starts with one.

Week 1 — Hiragana

46 characters

Ten characters a day for three days, sixteen on the fourth, then a day of nothing but retrieval. Hiragana is a grid, not an alphabet: five vowels, then each consonant row is that consonant plus the same five vowels in the same order. Learn the skeleton on day one and the rest is filling it in.

  • Day 1The vowels and the か row — あいうえお かきくけこ. The grid logic, which is the single most useful idea in the script. 10 characters
  • Day 2さ and た rows — さしすせそ たちつてと. Three of the four irregular readings live here: し shi, ち chi, つ tsu. 10 characters
  • Day 3な and は rows — なにぬねの はひふへほ. The fourth irregular: ふ fu. 10 characters
  • Day 4The rest — まみむめも やゆよ らりるれろ わを ん. Sixteen, but three rows are short. 16 characters
  • Day 5Consolidation and the look-alikes — all 46 mixed, then the traps: ね/れ/わ, る/ろ, ぬ/め, さ/き, は/ほ. Two questions settle almost all of them. Text 読み書き L1, pp. 296–299 · workbook pp. 121–125

Week 2 — Katakana

46 characters

Katakana writes foreign loanwords, which for anyone travelling is most of the words that matter: ホテル, タクシー, コンビニ, レストラン, パスポート. Same 46 sounds as hiragana, different shapes — and about twenty of those shapes you will already half-know on day 1.

  • Day 1The twenty you already know — katakana built from the same kanji as a hiragana: か→カ, き→キ, せ→セ, り→リ, and へ→ヘ, which is the identical shape. No memorising. 20 characters
  • Day 2The families — the remaining 22, sixteen of them one stroke from a day-1 shape: ク→タ→ケ, ノ→メ→ヌ→ス, フ→ワ→ヲ, ホ→オ→ネ. Only six need a mnemonic. The heaviest single session in the plan. 22 characters · Text pp. 24–27
  • Day 3Consolidation — all 42 mixed and drilled to accuracy, then read as whole words. No new characters. This day feels unproductive and is among the most important.
  • Day 4The four traps — シ ツ ン ソ, which cause more misreadings than the other 42 combined, and the one stroke-direction rule that settles all four. 4 characters
  • Day 5Katakana in the wild — whole travel words, not characters. Text 読み書き L2, pp. 300–303 · workbook pp. 128–132

Week 3 — The marks, the gate, and Genki begins

Marks, beats, gate — then Genki begins
  • Day 1The voicing marks — ゛ and ゜, in both scripts. か→が, さ→ざ, た→だ, は→ば/ぱ. One rule, 25 readings per script, no new shapes. workbook p. 126
  • Day 2Beats — small ゃゅょ (two characters, one beat), small っ (silent but counted), and ー. Plus は・へ・を read wa, e, o as particles. Most "I know the kana but still can't read" complaints are this day. workbook p. 127
  • Day 3Kana gate — all 104 readings, both scripts, timed. See the standard below.
  • Day 4Genki Lesson 1 begins.
  • Day 5Genki Lesson 1 continues.
The gate is accuracy, not speed

Two and a half weeks gets both scripts to accurate but slow. It will not make 92 brand-new shapes automatic, and nothing would. So the standard splits: 100% accuracy on all 104 readings, with speed still short of target. Sub-second recognition is built by the five-minute warm-up drill over the following weeks and typically arrives around Genki Lesson 3. Holding Genki hostage to kana speed costs three weeks and gains nothing — move on, and keep drilling in the warm-up.

Final session · Assessment

Week 9 Day 5  ·  Cold read まいにちのせいかつ pp. 304–307

No new material. Six tests: the three kana drills from the week-3 gate again, this time against the clock; all 43 kanji from Lessons 3–5 in isolation; twenty verbs into past and past negative; sixteen adjectives across both classes; thirteen grammar questions spanning every lesson; and a cold, timed, single-pass read of a passage you last saw three weeks ago.

The tests run oldest material first. That ordering is the diagnostic — a score on four-day-old material measures fluency strength and predicts little, while a score on four-week-old material measures storage strength and predicts everything. What breaks tells you what month three opens with.

The fifty-minute session

Fixed, so you never spend decision-making time on it. New material gets less than half the clock, which is deliberate: re-reading an explanation feels productive and builds almost nothing, while retrieving it cold is uncomfortable and builds almost everything.

ClockActivityWhy
0–7 minCold retrieval, book closed. Yesterday's material, plus one item from a previous lesson.Retrieval practice. The discomfort is the mechanism.
7–12 minKana speed drill. Stays at 5 minutes through Genki Lesson 3, then drops to 3.Automaticity, not accuracy — a different goal needing a different tool. This slot is where kana speed gets built after the gate.
12–33 minNew material from the textbook.The only block where difficulty is the enemy. Keep it easy here.
33–46 minProduction — workbook, or saying and writing your own sentences aloud.Recognising is not producing. You don't own a structure until you can build one.
46–50 minLog it. Three lines: what stuck, what didn't, what to re-test.Tomorrow's warm-up writes itself.
Day 1 of each week is different

Two days off means day 1's warm-up will feel bad. That is the spacing effect working, not evidence of failure — material retrieved after a gap is retained better than material retrieved while still warm. Give day 1's cold retrieval 12 minutes instead of 7, and don't re-read your notes first.

The reading protocol

This is what produces reading fluency, and it is the part that will feel most like a waste of time while you do it. It isn't. Gorsuch, Taguchi and Umehara ran a semester-long study on Japanese learners in which repeatedly re-reading the same passage with an audio model improved both reading speed and comprehension — and critically, the gains transferred to passages the learners had never seen.1 Re-reading one passage five times beats reading five passages once.

On day 5 of each lesson block, take that lesson's 読み書き passage and run:

  1. Listen only. Audio playing, eyes following the text. Don't read aloud, don't look anything up. You are installing a model of what it should sound like.
  2. Silent read. Mark every place you stalled. Now look up only those — not the whole passage.
  3. Chorus read. Read aloud along with the audio, keeping up. Where you fall behind is where your decoding is too slow.
  4. Solo read, timed. Aloud, alone, stopwatch running. Write the time in the margin.
  5. Solo read, timed again. Beat pass 4. Write that time down too.

Then close the book and answer the textbook's comprehension questions from memory. Keep the times — watching pass 5 of Lesson 5 come in faster than pass 5 of Lesson 3 is the clearest evidence of progress you will get in nine weeks.

1. Gorsuch, Taguchi & Umehara, "Repeated Reading for Japanese Language Learners," The Reading Matrix 15(2), 2015 — ERIC record EJ1075950. When you fall behind

You will, somewhere around Lesson 3. The rule is: drop breadth, never drop the day-5 reading session and never drop the warm-up. In priority order, what to cut from a block that is running over:

Cut firstCut last
Culture Notes pagesThe 読み書き passage (day 5)
Half the workbook sheets — do the odd-numbered onesThe cold-retrieval warm-up
Kanji writing; keep kanji readingGrammar points 1–3 of the lesson
Useful Expressions pagesVocabulary

If a whole lesson block slips by more than two sessions, re-cut the plan rather than carry a silent debt to the end. A nine-week plan that honestly delivers four lessons is worth more than one that nominally promises twelve and actually delivers three.

Lesson 6 is the one to protect. The て-form is the highest-leverage structure in elementary Japanese and the gateway to most of Genki II. It is irregular enough that it must be drilled to automaticity rather than understood once — give it the full block and expect it in your warm-ups for months. Lesson 6's kanji (東 西 南 北 口 出 右 左) are also the most immediately useful set in the book: 出口 is exit and 北口 is north gate, the characters on the signs in any Japanese station.

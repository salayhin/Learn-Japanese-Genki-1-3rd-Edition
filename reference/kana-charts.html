Reference · Kana

The Complete Kana

All 104 characters of both syllabaries, with the seven irregular readings marked. This is the chart to print and keep next to the textbook — the confusables card is its companion for the ones that look alike.

How to use this

Do not sit and memorise this page. Charts are for looking things up and for checking yourself against — reading a chart feels like learning and produces almost none. The learning happens in the lessons and the drills; this page exists so you always have ground truth to check against, and so you can see the shape of the whole system on one sheet.

Cells shaded like this are the ones whose reading does not follow the pattern of its row. There are seven. They cause a disproportionate share of beginner mistakes, so they are worth knowing as exceptions rather than discovering by accident.

Hiragana ひらがな 46 basic

The default script. Native Japanese words, all grammar — particles, verb endings, adjective endings — and any word whose kanji you haven't met yet. Roughly everything in a Genki sentence that isn't a kanji or a loanword is hiragana.

The seven oddities, and why they exist

  • し is shi, not si; ち is chi, not ti; つ is tsu, not tu; ふ is fu, not hu. Japanese sounds simply shifted over the centuries while the chart's logic stayed put. Four exceptions, learn them as exceptions.
  • を is read o, identical to お. It survives for exactly one job: marking the object of a verb. You will never see it inside a word.
  • ん is the only kana that is a bare consonant — every other one is a full syllable. It cannot start a word, which is the whole basis of the word game shiritori.

Katakana カタカナ 46 basic

Same 46 sounds, different shapes. Used for loanwords, foreign names, onomatopoeia and emphasis — which for a traveller means most of the words you will actually need on a sign: ホテル, タクシー, コンビニ, レストラン, パスポート. Note the grid is identical: learn the sound layout once, apply it twice.

Two katakana you can almost ignore

ヲ is vanishingly rare in modern Japanese — learn to recognise it and move on. ン, by contrast, is everywhere, because English words ending in -n are common loanwords: レストラン, パン, チェックイン. Do not let it blur with ソ — see week 2, day 4.

Voiced kana 濁音・半濁音 25

Not 25 new shapes — two small marks applied to rows you already know. ゛ (dakuten) voices the consonant; ゜ (handakuten) turns h into p. The shape underneath never changes.

The whole system in one line:

k → g  ·  s → z  ·  t → d  ·  h → b  ·  h → p

The catch: the four oddities carry over. し shi → じ ji (not zi), ち chi → ぢ ji, つ tsu → づ zu. So じ/ぢ sound identical and ず/づ sound identical. In practice you will almost always see じ and ず; the other two appear in a handful of words and you can safely treat them as curiosities.

Contracted sounds 拗音 33

An i-column kana followed by a small ゃ ゅ ょ. The two characters fuse into a single beat — this is the part that matters for reading aloud, and the part almost everyone gets wrong at first. きょ is kyo, one beat; it is not ki-yo, two beats.

Shaded rows again follow the shi/chi/ji pattern rather than the mechanical one: しゃ is sha, not sya; ちゅ is chu, not tyu; じょ is jo, not zyo. If you already know the four basic oddities, these follow for free.

Katakana goes further

Because katakana has to spell foreign sounds Japanese doesn't have, it uses extra small-vowel combinations that hiragana never does: ファ fa, フィ fi, ウェ we, ティ ti, チェ che, ジェ je, ヴ vu. You meet these constantly in travel vocabulary — チェックイン (check-in), カフェ (café), フォーク (fork). Don't memorise the set; just know that a small vowel after a consonant means "fuse them," and read it phonetically. Full table in the textbook, p. 27.

Both scripts share the same 104 sounds, so the real memory load is 92 shapes (46 × 2) plus two diacritic rules and one fusion rule — not 208 separate things. That reframing is the single most useful thing on this page.

And three kana that lie about their reading

Only when acting as grammatical particles — which, in a Genki sentence, is most of the time:

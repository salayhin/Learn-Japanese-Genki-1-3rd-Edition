Phase 0 · Week 2

Katakana, End to End

All 46 characters, in four sittings, sorted by how much work each one actually costs you. Twenty are nearly free, twenty-two hang off something you'll already know, and four need a rule of their own.

One part per day — don't do this in one sitting

Katakana writes Japan's foreign loanwords, which for a traveller is most of the words that matter: ホテル, タクシー, コンビニ, パスポート. Same 46 sounds as hiragana, different shapes.

Forty-six characters will not fit in one sitting, and trying is how people end up recognising none of them. Each part below is one day, with day 5 left free for reading whole words. Stop at the end of a part even if you feel fine. The overnight gap is doing work you cannot do by pushing harder.

Do week 1 first

This lesson assumes you can read hiragana — not perfectly, but reliably. Part 1 hands you twenty katakana for free by pointing at their hiragana twins, and that only works if the twins are solid. If you haven't done week 1, start there; it costs one week and saves more than one.

Why the order is what it is

The usual advice is to learn katakana with picture mnemonics, and Part 2 does exactly that where it's needed. But mnemonics are expensive — each one is a new thing to remember, and a story about a character is one more link in the chain between seeing a shape and hearing a sound. So the cheap routes come first:

  1. Point at knowledge you already have. About twenty katakana were carved from the same kanji as a hiragana you can already read. Free.
  2. Build new characters out of learned ones. Sixteen of the remaining twenty-two are one stroke away from something in Part 1. Cheap.
  3. Spend the mnemonic budget on the six that need it.
  4. Discriminate last. シ/ツ/ン/ソ come at the end because telling shapes apart is a different task from learning them, and it only works once both shapes are in your head.
Part 1 · Day 1

The Ones You Already Know

Twenty characters, no memorisation. Read each pair aloud once and move on.

Both scripts were built from kanji at around the same time by different groups. Hiragana came from cursive whole kanji, smoothed into a flowing shape. Katakana came from fragments — scribes annotating Chinese texts grabbed one piece of a character as shorthand. So when a hiragana and a katakana share a source kanji, the katakana usually looks like the hiragana with the curves taken out. That's a family resemblance you can see, not a fact to memorise.

The free one

An honest caveat

Some of these are strong (へ/ヘ, か/カ, き/キ) and some are faint (ひ/ヒ, ほ/ホ, む/ム). The shared origin is real in every case, but a faint resemblance is a weaker hook. If a pair doesn't click, don't force it — note it and it joins Part 2's mnemonic batch. The goal is cheap wins, not stubborn ones.

Part 2 · Day 2

The Families

Twenty-two characters. Sixteen are one stroke from something you learned in Part 1 — only six need a story.

This is the heaviest session in the whole plan, so it is organised to be as light as it can be. Most of these aren't new shapes; they're modifications of shapes you met on day 1. Learn the family, get three or four characters.

The ク family — add strokes

ク +slash→ タ +vertical→ ケ

ku · ta · ke — ク is the base: a narrow corner cutting in from the left. Put a slash through it and it's タ. Drop a long vertical through instead and it's ケ.

Watch these against ワ and ウ below — ク is narrower, and its top stroke cuts inward.

The ノ family — one stroke, then build

ノ +cross→ メ +roof→ ヌ  ·  ス

no · me · nu · su — ノ is a single diagonal, the simplest character in the script. Cross it and you have メ (which you already know, from め). Put a roof on メ and it's ヌ. Take the crossbar back off and it's ス — roof plus one leg.

The ワ family — フ plus a leg

フ +left→ ワ +bar→ ヲ

fu · wa · o — フ is a single bent stroke. Give it a left-hand leg and it's ワ. Put a bar through ワ and it's ヲ, which is rare enough that recognising it is plenty.

You already know ウ (u) from う — it's ワ wearing the little hat. That hat is the only difference, and it's the one to watch for.

The オ family — off a character you know

ホ −feet→ オ +hat→ ネ

o · ne — you learned ホ (ho) in Part 1. Take its two symmetrical feet away and what's left is オ. Put a small stroke on top of オ and you get ネ.

The テ family — one character, one difference

テ leg kicks left→ チ

chi — テ (te) you know from て. チ has the same two crossbars, but its vertical hooks to the left at the bottom instead of dropping straight from the centre. That hook is the whole distinction.

The レ family

レ +left leg→ ル

re · ru — レ is a single stroke like a tick. Add a second leg on the left and it becomes ル, whose right leg curls up at the end.

The two that are just numbers

ハ ←八     ミ ←三

ha · mi — ハ comes from 八, the kanji for eight, and looks exactly like it. ミ comes from 三, the kanji for three — three strokes, written fast and slanted. Two characters, two kanji you'll meet in Genki Lesson 3 anyway.

Part 3 · Day 3

Consolidation

No new characters. Forty-two shapes learned across two days need one day of nothing but retrieval.

This session looks like the least productive one in the plan and is among the most important. You have met these characters; you have not yet retrieved them cold, mixed, out of the order you learned them in. Recognition inside a lesson is not the same skill as recognition on a sign.

Run the drill below. Then run it again. Expect it to feel worse than Part 2 felt — that is the desirable difficulty doing its job, not evidence that Part 2 failed.

Now words

Reading happens at word level, not character level. Every word here uses only characters from Parts 1 and 2, plus the long-vowel bar ー.

If the drill went badly

Below about 80% is a signal to repeat this session tomorrow rather than push on to Part 4. Phase 0 has nine sessions and one of them is slack. Spending it here is a much better trade than arriving at the kana gate with forty shaky characters and four sharp ones.

Part 4 · Day 4

The Four Traps

シ ツ ン ソ — four characters that cause more misreadings than the other forty-two combined.

These come last on purpose. Telling シ from ツ is a discrimination task: it sharpens a distinction between two things you already hold. Attempted before you know the script, it just produces four shapes that all blur together.

Most people try to memorise them as four separate pictures and keep mixing them up, because as pictures they genuinely are nearly identical. The way out is to stop looking at the whole character and ask two narrow questions instead.

1. How many short strokes?
Two → シ or ツ. One → ン or ソ.

2. Which way does the long stroke sweep?
↗ Up, bottom-left to top-right → シ or ン.
↙ Down, top-right to bottom-left → ツ or ソ.

Why the sweep isn't arbitrary

A rule you understand survives a bad night's sleep; a rule you memorised does not. Each of these kept its source kanji's writing direction — which its hiragana cousin also kept. So the sweep is one you already know from hiragana:

し→シ
し finishes curving upward. So does シ.
つ→ツ
つ is drawn from the top, across and down. So is ツ.
ん→ン
ん finishes sweeping upward. So does ン.
そ→ソ
そ finishes sweeping down and left. So does ソ.
When the font fights you

Some display fonts flatten the sweep. Backup cue: on シ and ン the short strokes stack vertically down the left edge. On ツ and ソ they sit side by side along the top. Two cues, same answer.

What a good result looks like

Accurate and slow. That is correct at the end of week 2 and you should not be discouraged by it. Forty-six characters learned in four days will not be automatic in four days — nothing is. Speed is built by the five-minute warm-up drill across the following weeks and typically arrives around Genki Lesson 3.

Before week 3

Phase 0 · Week 2 · Day 5

Katakana in Whole Words

You can name all 46 characters. That is not yet reading. Today the shapes stop being shapes and start being words — and you discover that most katakana words are English wearing a disguise you can learn to see through.

Where this sits

Last day of week 2. You have done the four parts of katakana — the twenty free ones, the families, consolidation, and the traps. Today converts that into reading. Textbook 読み書き Lesson 2, pp. 300–303; workbook katakana sheets, workbook pp. 128–132.

The idea that changes everything

Reading is a word-level skill, not a character-level one. Fluent readers of any language don't assemble letters — they recognise chunks. You are still assembling, and that's correct for day 5; the point of today is to start the shift.

Katakana makes that shift unusually cheap, because of what katakana is for:

Most katakana words are foreign words you already know.
Decode the sounds, and the meaning often arrives by itself.

This is the opposite of kanji, where decoding gets you nothing without having learned the word. With katakana, sounding it out is the answer. ホテル is ho-te-ru — say it aloud at speed and you hear "hotel". That trick works on a large fraction of the katakana you'll meet on a sign.

How English becomes Japanese

The disguise follows rules. Japanese syllables are almost all consonant + vowel, with ン the only consonant allowed to stand alone. So an English word full of consonant clusters has to have vowels inserted to make it pronounceable:

strike
ス·ト·ラ·イ·ク
su · to · ra · i · ku

Six English letters, five Japanese beats. s can't stand alone so it becomes ス su; t becomes ト to; r takes the a that was already there; final k becomes ク ku.

Which vowel gets inserted isn't random:

abrupt stopッbed → ベッド, cup → コップ

The consequence nobody warns you about

Japanese words are longer than their English originals, because every consonant drags a vowel with it. Christmas is two syllables in English and five beats in Japanese, ク·リ·ス·マ·ス. McDonald's is three and becomes six, マ·ク·ド·ナ·ル·ド. So if a katakana word looks impossibly long for the word you suspect it is, that is a point in favour of your guess, not against it.

Why some words look like nonsense

Two things will break the decode-it-and-hear-it trick, and both are worth meeting now rather than in a shop.

1 · Japanese chops long loanwords

Borrowed words get trimmed, usually to about four beats. If a katakana word decodes to something that almost sounds like English but stops too early, this is why:

コンビニエンスストアコンビニconvenience store
スーパーマーケットスーパーsupermarket
エアコンディショナーエアコンair conditioner
スマートフォンスマホsmartphone
パーソナルコンピューターパソコンcomputer, PC
デパートメントストアデパートdepartment store

スマホ and パソコン show the trick at its most aggressive — pieces from both halves of the original, welded together. You cannot decode your way to those; they just have to be known.

2 · Some "English" words aren't English

Japanese has built its own English-looking vocabulary, called 和製英語 (wasei-eigo, "Japan-made English"). These decode perfectly and then mean something else — which makes them more dangerous than words you can't read at all.

マンションapartment Not a mansion — an ordinary flat in a concrete block.
コンセントpower outlet Nothing to do with consent.
バイキングbuffet From "Viking". Seen on restaurant signs constantly.
フロントreception desk The hotel front desk, not the front of anything.
クーラーair conditioning "Cooler", but it means the aircon.
サービスfree of charge "Service" — but on a menu it means it's on the house.

And not every katakana word came from English at all:

パンbreadPortuguese pão
アルバイトpart-time jobGerman Arbeit
ズボンtrousersFrench jupon
イクラsalmon roeRussian икра

Workbook and textbook

With the remaining minutes, open the book. Textbook 読み書き Lesson 2 (pp. 300–303) is Genki's own katakana section and ends with word-reading practice. The workbook's five katakana sheets (workbook pp. 128–132) are handwriting drills — use them as reading material, tracing once for shape memory rather than working them as a writing course. Writing kanji and kana from memory isn't part of this plan; recognising them at speed is.

What a good result looks like

You should finish today able to decode an unfamiliar katakana word given a few seconds, and to recognise perhaps a dozen on sight. Still slow, still deliberate. That's right. Word-level speed comes from meeting the same words repeatedly over the coming weeks, which the five-minute warm-up drill is for.

Before week 3

Do this in the real world: find katakana outside a textbook and read it. Product packaging in a supermarket, a café menu, the settings screen of any game or app switched to Japanese. Ten minutes of decoding real words beats another drill, because real text doesn't come pre-sorted into the character set you just studied.

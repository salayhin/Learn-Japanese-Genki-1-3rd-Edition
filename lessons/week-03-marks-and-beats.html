Phase 0 · Week 3

The Marks That Change Everything

You now know 92 shapes — both full scripts. This lesson adds 116 more readings without adding a single new shape, using five small marks and one idea: Japanese is counted in beats.

This one is worth two sittings

There is more here than fits comfortably in fifty minutes. Split it: Part 1 (the voicing marks) on day 1, Part 2 (beats) on day 2, with the kana gate on day 3. Part 2 is the more important half and the one that actually decides whether you can read aloud — do not let it get squeezed into the last ten minutes of a tired session.

The whole system, before we start

Nearly every learner meets が, きゃ and っ as three unrelated pieces of trivia. They are not. They are five marks doing two jobs:

MarkJobEffect on beats
゛Voices the consonant: k→g, s→z, t→d, h→bNone
゜Turns h into pNone
ゃゅょFuses with the previous kanaTwo characters become one beat
っDoubles the next consonantSilent, but takes a full beat
ーExtends the previous vowel (katakana)Takes a full beat

The first two add sounds. The last three change timing — and timing is what separates reading aloud from reciting characters.

Part 1 · The voicing marks

Two dashes in the top-right corner voice the consonant. The shape underneath never changes, and the mark works identically in both scripts — so this is genuinely one rule, not fifty new characters.

か → が
カ → ガ
ka → ga
さ → ざ
サ → ザ
sa → za
た → だ
タ → ダ
ta → da
は → ば
ハ → バ
ha → ba
は → ぱ
ハ → パ
ha → pa

The rule in one line

k → g  ·  s → z  ·  t → d  ·  h → b  ·  h + ゜ → p

は is the busy one: it is the only row that takes both marks, giving ha → ba with ゛ and ha → pa with ゜. Everything else takes only ゛. Say the pairs aloud with a hand on your throat — the voiced one buzzes, the plain one doesn't. That is literally the entire distinction.

The three that misbehave

The four irregular readings you already know (し shi, ち chi, つ tsu, ふ fu) carry their irregularity through the voicing rule:

し → じ
shi → ji
ち → ぢ
chi → ji
つ → づ
tsu → zu
ふ → ぶ
fu → bu

So じ and ぢ sound identical, and ず and づ sound identical. In practice you will see じ and ず almost every time — ぢ and づ survive in a handful of compound words and you can treat them as curiosities to recognise, not rules to apply.

Part 2 · Beats

This is the half that matters most, and the half most self-learners never quite internalise. English is stress-timed: we squash and stretch syllables around stressed ones. Japanese is beat-timed — every unit gets roughly equal length, like a metronome. Get the beat count wrong and you are saying a different word, even with every sound correct.

One kana = one beat.
Except for the three marks below, which is the whole reason they need a lesson.

Small ゃ ゅ ょ — two characters, one beat

An i-column kana plus a small ゃゅょ fuse into a single beat. Written small, they merge; written full size, they don't — and that is a real difference in meaning, not a nicety:

きょう
kyo · u
2 beats — "today"
きよう
ki · yo · u
3 beats — "dexterous"

Same three characters. The only difference is the size of the middle one. If you read きょう as three beats, you have said a different word.

Small っ — silent, but still a beat

The small tsu makes no sound of its own. It doubles the following consonant and holds a beat of stopped breath. Beginners almost always swallow it, which is why their Japanese sounds rushed:

きて
ki · te
2 beats — "come"
きって
ki · (hold) · te
3 beats — "postage stamp"

The grey bar is the silent beat. It is the sound of an English speaker saying "black cat" and pausing fractionally on the k — you already make this sound, you just don't count it.

ー — the long-vowel bar

Katakana only. It holds the previous vowel for one more beat. Hiragana does the same job by doubling the vowel instead (おう, えい):

コーヒー
ko · o · hi · i
4 beats — "coffee"
ビール
bi · i · ru
3 beats — "beer"
Why a traveller should care

Because the words you will most need are full of these marks. チェックイン is five beats. スーツケース is six. きっぷ (ticket) is three, with a silent one in the middle. Say them with the wrong beat count at a hotel desk and you will get a polite, baffled pause — the sounds were right, the rhythm wasn't.

Count the beats

The single most useful drill on this page. Say each word aloud, tap the beats on the desk, then answer.

Katakana goes one step further

Katakana has to spell sounds Japanese doesn't natively have, so it extends the small-vowel trick past ゃゅょ to the ordinary vowels. Hiragana never does this. You don't need to memorise the set — just recognise that a small vowel means "fuse with the character before it" and read it phonetically:

Read them cold

Mixed voiced kana and ゃゅょ combinations, both scripts, no chart. Type the romaji and press Enter.

Before the gate

Primary source: the textbook's own treatment — GENKI I, Japanese Writing System, pp. 20–27. It is compact, authoritative, and it is the version your course will keep referring back to. Ten minutes.

Then: Day 3 is the kana gate — all 104 readings in both scripts, timed. The standard is 100% accuracy; speed will still be short of target and that is expected, because it is built by the five-minute warm-up drill over the following weeks. Put the complete charts somewhere you can glance at them, and use the drills on this page, on week 1 and on week 2 as your warm-ups. Genki Lesson 1 starts on day 4.

What you have after this: both scripts, all 104 readings each, and the timing rules that turn characters into speech. That is the entire writing system minus kanji — and kanji don't start until Genki Lesson 3.

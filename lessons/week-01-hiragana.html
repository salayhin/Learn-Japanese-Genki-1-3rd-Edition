Phase 0 · Week 1

Hiragana, End to End

All 46 characters in five sittings, from nothing. Hiragana is the default script of Japanese — the grammar, the particles, the verb endings, and any word whose kanji you haven't met. It is the first thing to learn and the last thing you stop using.

One part per day — don't do this in one sitting

Forty-six characters will not fit in one session, and trying is how people end up recognising none of them. Each part below is one weekday. Stop at the end of a part even if you feel fine. The overnight gap is doing work you cannot do by pushing harder.

The one idea to take from today

Hiragana is a grid, not an alphabet. There are five vowel sounds. Every other character is a consonant plus one of those five vowels, always in the same order: a, i, u, e, o. So the script isn't 46 unrelated things — it's five sounds and ten rows that reuse them.

Faded rows are still to come. The two full rows are today's ten characters.

Once you know the order a-i-u-e-o, you can predict what a row contains before you learn it: the s row will be sa-shi-su-se-so, the m row ma-mi-mu-me-mo. You are not memorising 46 sounds — you're memorising 46 shapes and attaching them to a pattern you already hold.

Part 1 · Day 1

The Vowels and the か Row

Ten characters. The five vowels are the backbone of everything that follows.

Hooks below are starting points, not gospel. A mnemonic you invent yourself is always stickier than one you were handed — if a better one occurs to you, use yours.

The five vowels
あaA capital A with a curve looping through it.
いiTwo strokes leaning together — like two eels side by side.
うuA tick and a curve: a face in profile going "oo".
えeA bar, then a zigzag body — an exotic bird with a long neck.
おoLike あ, plus a small flick top-right. That flick is the whole difference.
The か row — k + each vowel
かkaA kaite (kite) with a tail and a tick.
きkiA key: two teeth on a stem.
くkuA beak opening — a cuckoo.
けkeA keg: upright barrel with a tap on the left.
こkoTwo short bars — two commas stacked.

Part 2 · Day 2

The さ and た Rows

Ten characters, and the first place the grid's logic breaks.

Four characters don't follow the pattern

By the grid, the s row should read sa-si-su-se-so and the t row ta-ti-tu-te-to. Three of them don't: し is shi, ち is chi, つ is tsu. The fourth exception, ふ fu, arrives tomorrow. Japanese sounds drifted over centuries while the chart's logic stayed put. Four exceptions in the whole script — learn them as exceptions and move on.

The さ row
さsaLike き but with one crossbar instead of two.
しshiA single hook — a fishing line. Not si.
すsuA bar, then a vertical straight through a small loop.
せseA bar with a vertical and a hook — like a seesaw frame.
そsoA zigzag dropping down-left.
The た row
たtaA cross on the left, two marks on the right.
ちchiさ flipped — the curve opens right. Reads chi, not ti.
つtsuA single horizontal wave — a tsunami curl. Not tu.
てteOne stroke, bending down and left.
とtoA vertical with a toe-tick poking out.

Part 4 · Day 4

The Remaining Rows

Sixteen characters, but three of these rows are short — や has three, わ has two, ん stands alone.

The ま row
まmaTwo crossbars, then a loop at the bottom.
みmiA loop with a tail — contains a shape like the number 21.
むmuA low loop with a flick — a cow saying moo.
めmeLike ぬ but the tail stops short — no loop.
もmoA hook crossed by two bars — a fishing hook with more line.
The や row — three only
やyaA loop, a crossbar, a diagonal leg.
ゆyuA fish shape with a line through it.
よyoA vertical crossed once, with a loop at the bottom.
The ら row
らraA tick above a curve.
りriTwo strokes, the right one long and hooking left.
るruEnds in a tight closed curl. ru is round.
れreLike ね but the tail kicks outward — no loop.
ろroLike る but the tail runs off open.
The last three
わwaLike ね/れ but the tail curves inward and stops.
をoRead o, same as お. Used only as the object particle — never inside a word.
んnThe only bare consonant — every other kana is a full syllable. Cannot start a word.

Part 5 · Day 5

Consolidation and the Look-Alikes

No new characters. Retrieval, then the shapes that blur into each other.

You have met all 46. You have not yet retrieved them cold, mixed, out of the order you learned them in — and recognition inside a lesson is a different skill from recognition on a page. Run the drill below before reading further.

The two questions

Hiragana's confusable characters aren't randomly confusable. Almost all of them split along one of two features. Stop comparing whole shapes and ask:

Question 1

Does the final stroke close into a loop, or stay open?

This one question separates ね from れ, る from ろ, and ぬ from め — three of the most common beginner misreadings, all one rule.

Question 2

How many horizontal crossbars?

This separates さ from き, and は from ほ. In both pairs, the character with the extra bar is the less common one.

Loop or no loop

ねneloop Tail curls round and closes on itself.
れreopen · flicks out Same body, tail kicks outward right.
わwaopen · curves in Same body, tail curves inward and stops.
るruloop Ends in a tight closed curl.
ろroopen Identical path, tail runs off. ru is round.
ぬnuloop Crossed body, then a closed loop.
めmeopen Same crossed body, tail stops short.
One hook for all three pairs

The looped one of each pair is ね / る / ぬ — ne, ru, nu. All three are n-or-r sounds ending in a knot. Tie the knot, get the n. It's arbitrary, which is exactly why it works: the characters are arbitrary too, so an artificial hook beats trying to reason about them.

Count the bars

さsaone bar Single crossbar, curve opening left.
きkitwo bars Same shape, one extra bar. The key's extra tooth.
ちchione bar · mirrored さ flipped — the curve opens right.
はhaone bar Vertical, one crossbar, right-hand loop.
ほhotwo bars は with a hat.
まmatwo bars · bottom loop Two bars, but the loop is at the bottom, not the side.

The leftovers

おoNearly the same, plus a flick top-right.
すsuVertical runs straight through a small loop.
むmuLoop sits low, with a separate flick right.
いiTwo short strokes, both leaning the same way.
りriRight stroke is long and hooks left.

What a good result looks like

Accurate and slow. That is correct at the end of week 1 and shouldn't discourage you. Forty-six characters learned in five days will not be automatic in five days; nothing is. Speed comes from the five-minute warm-up drill over the following weeks and typically arrives around Genki Lesson 3. If you're below about 80% accuracy, repeat day 5 at the start of week 2 rather than starting katakana — the week-2 lesson leans directly on solid hiragana.

Day 1

Vocabulary, Dialogue, and Numbers

Input day, plus the one piece of Lesson 2 that is genuinely hard for an English speaker and has nothing to do with grammar.

  1. Vocabulary, pp. 58–60. Read once with the audio. This list is full of concrete objects — bags, watches, shoes, umbrellas — which makes it far easier to hold than Lesson 1's abstract nouns. The starred words appear in the dialogue.
  2. Dialogue, pp. 56–57. Listen twice, reading along. It's a shop transaction, so you'll hear the shape of the whole lesson before you learn any of it.
  3. Numbers, p. 67 and the Expression Notes at p. 65. Details below — do this part properly, it pays off immediately.

Japanese counts in ten-thousands

English chunks big numbers in thousands: thousand, million, billion — each one a thousand times the last. Japanese chunks in ten-thousands, using a unit English doesn't have: まん (10,000).

10,000 = いちまん and everything above it is counted in man, not thousands
20,000 → にまんniman 2 × 10,000 — not "twenty thousand"
1,000,000 → ひゃくまんhyakuman 100 × 10,000 — a million is "a hundred man"

This is not a vocabulary problem, it's a re-chunking problem: you have to stop hearing "twenty-thousand" and start hearing "two-man". It takes a few weeks and it is the main reason beginners freeze at a till. Practise by reading prices aloud.

One to memorise flat

10,000 is いちまん, not just まん — even though 10 is plain じゅう, 100 is plain ひゃく, and 1,000 is plain せん. まん alone is wrong. The book flags this with an asterisk on p. 67 for good reason.

The hundreds and thousands that change sound

Counters beginning with h (like ひゃく) shift after 3, 6 and 8. Counters beginning with s (like せん) shift after 3 and 8. Six irregulars in total — everything else is regular:

300さんびゃく
600ろっぴゃく
800はっぴゃく
3,000さんぜん
8,000はっせん
10,000いちまん

Full table at p. 67, and the general counter table at pp. 380–381. These six are the ones to drill; the rest follow the pattern you'd guess.

Drill the numbers

Three-way, not two-way

English has this and that. Japanese has three: near me, near you, and far from both. The middle one is the one English speakers forget — if the thing is in the other person's hand, it is それ, even though in English you'd still say "that".

これ or この?

Same meaning, different grammar. これ stands alone as a noun. この cannot stand alone — it must be glued to a noun before the sentence can continue:

これは いくらですか。kore wa ikura desu ka How much is this one?
このとけいは いくらですか。kono tokee wa ikura desu ka How much is this watch?

So これ takes は directly; この needs a noun first, and the noun takes は. Saying このは… is not a thing.

Politeness: don't point at people with これ

The この series is fine for people — あのがくせい, "that student over there". The これ series used of a person can come across as impolite, because it treats them as a thing. The book notes it's acceptable when referring back to someone already mentioned, or pointing at a photo. Use あの + noun and you're always safe.

Question words can't be topics

どれ and どの take が, not は: どのがくせいが にほんじんですか. You haven't formally met が yet — it arrives in Lesson 8 — so for now just learn this as a fact about question words. The why is that は marks known, shared information, and a question word is by definition the unknown part.

Check it

Day 3

Places, and だれの

Textbook p. 62. A short day — take the spare minutes for yesterday's grid.

The third column of the grid: ここ / そこ / あそこ / どこ. Note the odd one out — あそこ has an extra syllable where you'd expect あこ.

This gives you the single most useful question in a strange city:

すみません。ゆうびんきょくは どこですか。 sumimasen. yuubinkyoku wa doko desu ka Excuse me. Where is the post office?
あそこです。asoko desu It's over there.

Answers more specific than "over there" — next to, behind, in front of — come in Lesson 4.

だれの — whose

You already know の joins two nouns. Put the question word だれ (who) in the first slot and you're asking who something belongs to:

だれの + Noun whose ～
これは だれの かばんですか。kore wa dare no kaban desu ka Whose bag is this?
それは ソラさんの かばんです。sore wa Sora san no kaban desu That's Sora's bag.

Look at how the answer mirrors the question: これ becomes それ, because the thing has moved from near the speaker to near the listener. That swap is the grid doing real work.

Check it

Day 4

も, じゃないです, and ね / よ

Textbook pp. 62–64. Three small things that transform how natural you sound.

1 · も — "too"

A は X です。   B も X です。 A is X. B is X too.

Two things about も catch English speakers, and both come from the same source — in English "too" floats to the end of the sentence, in Japanese it is welded to the item it's about.

たけしさんは にほんじんです。Takeshi san wa nihonjin desu Takeshi is Japanese.
ゆいさんも にほんじんです。Yui san mo nihonjin desu Yui is Japanese too.
  1. も replaces は. It does not join it. ゆいさんはも is not a thing.
  2. It attaches to the likewise item. Above, Yui gets も because Yui is the one that matches something already said. Put it elsewhere and you change the meaning.

2 · じゃないです — negation

To negate X は Y です, swap です for じゃないです:

やまださんは がくせいです。Yamada san wa gakusee desu Mr. Yamada is a student.
やまださんは がくせいじゃないです。…gakusee ja nai desu Mr. Yamada is not a student.

There are three registers of the same thing. Recognise all three; produce the first:

FormRegister
じゃないですColloquial. Use this one.
じゃありませんMore conservative speech.
ではありませんFormal; the right choice in writing.

じゃ is simply a contraction of では — which is why the formal version looks longer. Same word, different amount of smoothing.

Two traps

They need a noun. Neither です nor じゃない can stand alone. Answering いいえ、じゃないです is ungrammatical — you must repeat the noun: いいえ、にほんじんじゃないです.

This does not negate adjectives. たかいです ("it's expensive") cannot become たかいじゃないです, because たかい is not a noun. Adjectives have their own conjugation and it arrives in Lesson 5. Until then, avoid negating them.

3 · ね and よ

Sentence-final tags that say something about you and the listener rather than about the facts. Neither is optional-sounding once you're used to them — Japanese without them sounds flat and slightly cold.

…ですね。…desu ne ね — seeking agreement. "…right?" / "…isn't it?"
…ですよ。…desu yo よ — telling them something they may not know.

Use ね when you expect the listener already shares your view, and よ when you're supplying information and want them to take it from you. Getting them backwards is a small but very audible error: よ where ね belongs can sound like you're correcting someone.

Check it

Day 5

Workbook

Workbook pp. 25–31 — one sheet per grammar point, numbered to match the textbook.

Production day. Everything so far has been recognition; these sheets make you build sentences, which is a different and harder skill. Work in order — they're sequenced to match the grammar numbering, so if a sheet stumps you the explanation is one page-flip away.

The set covers numbers, the two こそあど halves, places and だれの, then も with じゃないです, finishing with こたえましょう (written questions) and きくれんしゅう (listening, three or four tasks).

Don't skip the listening sheet

It's the only part of this lesson that makes you decode Japanese at someone else's pace rather than your own — which is exactly the situation a real shop puts you in. If it's too fast, use OTO Navi's speed control and the three-second rewind rather than giving up on it.

Day 6

Retrieval and a Shopping Roleplay

Closed book, then build the whole transaction end to end.

Re-run the quizzes above cold first — they reshuffle, so a second run is a new test. Then assemble the thing this lesson was for.

The transaction

Every line uses only Lesson 1 and 2 grammar. Say both parts aloud, swapping roles, until you can do it without reading:

  1. すみません。Excuse me. (Gets attention; also means "sorry".)
  2. このものは いくらですか。How much is this ___? この + noun.
  3. ねだんえんです。It's ___ yen. Watch the sound changes.
  4. あれも ねだんえんですか。Is that one over there ___ yen too? も, not は.
  5. いいえ、ねだんえんじゃないです。No, it's not ___ yen. Repeat the noun — じゃないです can't stand alone.
  6. たかいですね。That's expensive, isn't it. ね seeks agreement.
  7. じゃあ、これを ください。Then I'll take this one, please.

Asking for things: ください and おねがいします

From the Expression Notes at p. 65 — both mean roughly "please give me X", and the difference is worth knowing:

PhraseWhen
(〜を)ください Requesting a concrete item. The everyday default.
(〜を)おねがいします Slightly more upscale for an object — common when ordering food. Also the one that works for abstract requests: a repair, an explanation, someone's understanding.
(〜を)どうぞ Not a request — this is what the other person says when offering something to you. "Here you are."

Mixed retrieval

Finish with the classroom page

p. 81, Useful Expressions — きょうしつ, phrases for a classroom. Read it, don't drill it. Several of them are worth having even without a class: "how do you say ～ in Japanese?", "please say it again", "I don't understand". Those are survival phrases for any conversation with a native speaker.

And the money

p. 66, Culture Notes — Japanese currency. Worth two minutes: the note that Japan still runs substantially on cash, and that small shops and restaurants may not take cards even in big cities, is practical information rather than trivia.

What you can do now

  • Point at anything and ask what it is, whose it is, and what it costs.
  • Distinguish near-me, near-you, and over-there — a three-way split English doesn't have.
  • Say a price up to the tens of thousands, and understand one said to you.
  • Negate a noun sentence, and say "this one too".
  • Soften or assert a statement with ね and よ, which is the first thing in this course that makes you sound like a person rather than a textbook.

Read prices out loud in the wild. Any price you see this week, anywhere, convert it to Japanese in your head. The まん re-chunking only becomes automatic through repetition, and it's the thing most likely to strand you at a till.

Final session · Week 9 · Day 5

The Assessment

The last session in the plan, and the only one with nothing new in it. Nine weeks of work gets measured today. Nothing is gated behind the result — there is no next lesson to unlock — so this exists for exactly one purpose: to write the syllabus for month three out of evidence instead of guesswork.

Rules of engagement

Nothing open, and no warming up. Don't re-read Lesson 5 first, don't glance at the kanji list, don't run a practice round. A test you prepare for measures your last ten minutes; a cold test measures the nine weeks. The whole value of this page is destroyed by five minutes of revision, and the temptation to do it is strongest on the material you are least sure of — which is precisely the material you most need a true number for.

What changed since the kana gate

In week 3 the standard was accuracy, and explicitly not speed. Today that reverses. Accuracy is now assumed, and speed is the measurement.

That isn't a stylistic preference. Reading comprehension runs on working memory, and working memory is small. Every character you have to decode consumes some of it, and whatever is spent on decoding is unavailable for meaning. Automatic recognition doesn't make you a slightly faster reader — it is what makes you a reader at all. So the numbers below are the point, not a footnote to the scores.

How to read your own scoresheet

Before you start, one thing about the order of the tests, because it determines what the numbers mean.

The six tests run oldest material first, newest last. That is deliberate, and it makes the scores non-comparable with each other on purpose:

~6 weeks oldKana — finished in week 3, drilled daily since. Should be automatic.
~4 weeks oldLesson 3 verbs and kanji. Old enough that the score means something.
~2 weeks oldLesson 4 — past tense, existence, the days of the week.
4 days oldLesson 5 — adjectives, 好き, counting. Still warm.
A high score on Lesson 5 predicts almost nothing

You finished it four days ago. It will feel fluent, and it will score well, and neither fact tells you whether you will still have it in March. What you are measuring there is fluency strength — how readily something comes to hand right now — which decays fast and is a notoriously bad guide to whether anything was actually learned.

The tests worth staring at are the old ones. A clean score on Lesson 3's verbs, four weeks after you last studied them deliberately, is evidence of storage strength, and storage strength is the only kind that predicts anything. So read the scoresheet top-down, and weight it accordingly: the first numbers are the informative ones, and the last number is the flattering one.

Six tests

Run them in order, in one sitting, and write each result down as you go. Record your first attempt at every test — re-running afterwards as practice is useful, but a second score measures something different, because you have just seen the answers.

~6 weeks old Test 1

Kana — the same three drills as the gate

Identical character sets to week 3, day 3, so your times are directly comparable. Back then the targets were 55s, 60s and 40s and speed was explicitly not the standard. They are tighter now, and they are the standard.

Dig out your week-3 numbers before you start. The gap between then and now is six weeks of five-minute warm-ups, and it is the cleanest measurement of deliberate practice on this whole page.

~4 to 1 weeks old Test 2

Kanji — all 43, in isolation

Fifteen from Lesson 3 (numbers, yen, time), fourteen from Lesson 4 (days of the week), fourteen from Lesson 5. Shuffled, and stripped of the words they normally sit in — which makes this considerably harder than meeting them in a sentence, and is the point.

Any reading the book taught for a character counts. This is the thinnest material on the page, so the standard allows misses.

~4 weeks old Test 3

Verbs against the clock

Twenty verbs, ten into the past affirmative and ten into the past negative, across all three groups. The groups are shuffled together, because choosing the right rule is the actual skill and you never practise choosing by doing ten of one kind in a row.

This is the most important test on the page. Verb conjugation is the load-bearing wall of everything after it — the て-form, the plain forms, the potential, the passive all start from these stems — and it is old enough now for the score to be honest.

4 days old Test 4

Adjectives against the clock

Eight into the past affirmative, eight into the present negative, with い- and な-adjectives mixed throughout. What is being tested is not the endings but the class — whether you know, without stopping, which of the two rules a given word takes.

Expect to do well. Note the caveat above about what that does and doesn't mean.

all nine weeks Test 5

Grammar — thirteen questions, all five lessons

Particles and structures, which no timed drill can reach. Drills test whether you can produce a form; these test whether you know which form the sentence needs, and that is where almost all real errors live.

No clock on this one. Take the time to think — a rushed wrong answer here tells you nothing you can act on.

~3 weeks since last read Test 6

Cold read — まいにちのせいかつ

Textbook pp. 304–307, the Lesson 3 reading. You last worked on it in week 7, and you ran it five times with audio that day.

One pass. Timed. Nothing looked up, no audio, no re-reading a line you stumbled on. Then close the book and answer its comprehension questions from memory. Write down both the time and how many you got.

What this number is, and what it isn't

It has no exact precedent. Your pass-4 and pass-5 times that day came after you had already read the passage three times with an audio model, so they measure something this doesn't. Don't line them up and conclude you've got worse — you are comparing a cold first pass against a warm fifth one.

What the comparison can tell you is whether the gain survived. If today's cold read lands anywhere near that week-7 pass-5 time, the repeated reading consolidated into something durable rather than a within-session effect — which is precisely the transfer the Gorsuch, Taguchi and Umehara study measured. If it is dramatically slower, the gain was local to the session. Either way this becomes your baseline, and month three's job is to beat it.

What your result means

Find the lines that match. More than one can apply, and none of them is a failure — every branch below ends in a specific thing to do, which is the only output this page was built to produce.

Kana under target, kanji and verbs at standard

The foundation is done and it is holding. Month three opens with Lesson 6 and the て-form. Drop the kana warm-up entirely and give those five minutes to kanji — it is now the slowest-growing thing you own.

Kana accurate but still over target

Recognition works; automaticity hasn't arrived. This is the one result that should change your daily routine rather than your syllabus, because slow decoding taxes every other thing on this list. Keep the five-minute warm-up drill and expect another three or four weeks. It does arrive, and when it does you will notice it in reading speed rather than in the drill.

Kanji well below 39, everything else clean

The expected shape of this result, and not a problem — it is a consequence of how few kanji Genki introduces in its first five lessons. Add a standing daily kanji block in month three and stop treating them as a by-product of lessons. Genki I has 145 in total; you have 43, which means the rate has to roughly triple, and that only works as its own habit.

Verbs slow or scattered, adjectives fast

The most important result on the page, and the easiest one to talk yourself out of. It is not evidence that adjectives are easier — it is the four-week gap versus the four-day one, which is to say it is exactly the pattern of something that was learned and then not retrieved. Put verb conjugation into every warm-up for the next month, interleaved and cold. The て-form is built on these stems, and starting Lesson 6 on shaky ones makes a hard lesson much harder.

Grammar below 11, drills all clean

You can produce forms but you're choosing the wrong ones — which means the gap is in particles, not conjugation. Go back through your misses and sort them by lesson. If they cluster on に/で or は/が, that is the Lesson 4 particle flip not having settled, and it is worth one deliberate re-read.

Cold read far slower than the week-7 pass 5, and comprehension gone

Honest interpretation: decay, which is what spacing exists to prevent, and this is the first time the plan has asked you to retrieve anything across a three-week gap. The fix is not to re-study Lesson 3. Add one old passage to every week in month three — a single cold re-read of something you last saw a month ago. It costs ten minutes a week and it is the difference between a course you completed and a language you have.

Month three

Whatever the numbers said, the shape of the next month is the same. Five things, in priority order:

  1. Lesson 6 — the て-form

    The highest-leverage single item in elementary Japanese, and the thing this plan had to leave out. It joins clauses, makes requests, grants permission and builds the progressive — most of what you currently can't say is behind it. Give it the same treatment Lesson 3 got: seven sessions, not five.

  2. A standing kanji block

    Fifteen minutes, every session, separate from the lesson. This is the one habit that doesn't emerge naturally from working through Genki, and the one whose absence compounds fastest.

  3. Keep the five-pass protocol, and add extensive reading beside it

    The protocol is intensive reading — one passage, deeply. It needs a counterweight: lots of text, far below your level, read once and not analysed. Tadoku's free graded readers start at a level you can already handle, and reading something easy end to end is a different and necessary skill.

  4. One cold re-read a week

    Whatever today's test 6 told you. A passage from a month ago, read once, timed. It is the cheapest insurance against everything above quietly decaying.

  5. Decide about the JLPT

    N5 is roughly Genki I plus a little. On this pace you'd be ready for a July sitting rather than December. That is worth deciding now rather than later, because a booked exam changes how much test-format reading is worth folding in — and an unbooked one tends to stay unbooked.

Nine weeks. Two writing systems and 104 readings in each, five Genki lessons, 43 kanji, around 200 words, verbs and adjectives in two tenses and both polarities, and four timed readings of real Japanese text. On day one none of this page would have been legible to you — not the title, not a single character in any drill on it. That is worth stopping on for a moment before you plan month three, because it is the kind of progress that is invisible from the inside and obvious from any distance at all.

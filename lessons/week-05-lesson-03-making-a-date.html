Vocabulary and Dialogue

Input only. Resist the urge to start analysing the verbs — that's tomorrow.

Learn each verb as a pair, starting today

The single most useful habit in this lesson: never memorise a verb's dictionary form alone. Memorise it with its ます form — いく — いきます, たべる — たべます. The pair tells you which group the verb belongs to, which is information you will need for every conjugation from here to the end of Genki II. Learning them separately means learning them twice.

Day 2

The Three Verb Groups

Textbook pp. 86–88. The heaviest single day in the course. Take it slowly.

Every Japanese verb belongs to one of three groups, and the group decides how it conjugates. Get this wrong and every later form — past, negative, て-form, potential — comes out wrong too. Get it right and they all follow.

る-verbs

base ending in e or i  +  る
たべる · ねる · おきる · みる

The base is what's left when you remove る: tabe, ne, oki, mi — all ending in e or i. So every る-verb ends in -eru or -iru.

う-verbs

base ending in a consonant  +  u
のむ · よむ · はなす · きく · いく · かえる

nom+u, yom+u, kik+u. The base ends in a consonant, and u is the suffix.

Irregular — there are only two

する  ·  くる
する · くる · べんきょうする

する is hugely productive: attach it to a noun and you get a verb. べんきょうする (to study) is noun + する, and there are hundreds more like it. Learn する once, get all of them.

The trap: かえる

かえる (to go home) ends in -eru, so it looks exactly like a る-verb. It is not. Its base is kaer- and the r belongs to the base, not the suffix — so it's a う-verb, giving かえります, never ×かえます. This is why the book tells you to memorise the pair rather than guess from the shape.

How much you can tell from the ending

If a verb ends in る, the sound before it narrows things down but never settles them completely:

Ends in…Then it is…
-aru or -oruDefinitely a う-verb.
-uruIrregular, or a う-verb.
-iru or -eruProbably a る-verb — but can be a う-verb, as かえる shows.

The ます form

Now the payoff. Each group forms the polite present tense differently:

GroupDictionaryAffirmativeNegative
る-verbたべるたべますたべません
る-verbみるみますみません
う-verbのむのみますのみません
う-verbきくききますききません
う-verbかえるかえりますかえりません
irregularするしますしません
irregularくるきますきません

る-verbs: drop る, add ます. That's all.

う-verbs: the final u becomes i, then add ます. In kana terms you move up one row in the same column: む → み, く → き, す → し, る → り. The hiragana grid you learned in week 1 is doing real grammatical work here — this is the first place it pays off.

What "present tense" actually means

A misleading name. The ます form covers two things, and neither is "happening right now":

わたしは よく テレビを みます。watashi wa yoku terebi o mimasu Habitual — I often watch TV.
あした きょうとに いきます。ashita Kyooto ni ikimasu Future — I'll go to Kyoto tomorrow.

"I am watching TV at this moment" needs a different form, 〜ている, which arrives in Lesson 7. Until then, don't try to say it.

Day 3

Particles

Textbook pp. 88–89. Four small words that carry the grammar English carries with word order.

English says who-did-what-to-what by position: "the dog bit the man" differs from "the man bit the dog". Japanese marks it instead, with particles attached to each noun — which is why Japanese word order can be flexible while English cannot.

をdirect object The thing acted on. Read o, never wo.
でplace of action Where the event happens.
にgoal, or time Two jobs: where movement ends, and when something happens.
へgoal of movement Read e. Only direction — never time.
コーヒーを のみます。koohii o nomimasu I drink coffee. — coffee is what's affected
としょかんで ほんを よみます。toshokan de hon o yomimasu I read books at the library. — で marks the place
うちに かえります。uchi ni kaerimasu I'll go home. — に marks the destination
にちようびに いきます。nichiyoobi ni ikimasu I'll go on Sunday. — に marks the time
に or へ?

へ can replace に for a destination, with movement verbs like いく, くる, かえる. So うちに かえります and うちへ かえります are both fine.

へ can never replace に for time. ×にちようびへ is wrong. So if you're unsure, に is the safer choice — it does both jobs.

Two particles that lie about their spelling

を is read o and へ is read e — exactly the rule you met in week 3. Lesson 3 is where it stops being trivia and starts appearing in every sentence you write.

Approximate times

Swap に for ごろ (or use ごろに) to say "about":

じゅういちじに ねます。juuichiji ni nemasu I go to bed at eleven.
じゅういちじごろ ねます。juuichiji goro nemasu I go to bed at about eleven.

Check it

Day 4

Time, Frequency, and Word Order

Textbook pp. 90–91. Mostly rules about when not to use に.

When time expressions take に

This trips up nearly everyone, because the rule isn't "all times take に". It splits on whether the expression is an absolute point or a relative one:

に requiredNo に
Days of the week — にちようびに Relative to now — きょう, あした
Clock times — じゅうじに Regular intervals — まいにち, まいばん
Months — くがつに いつ (when)

The logic: に pins an event to a fixed point on the calendar or clock. "Tomorrow" isn't a fixed point — it depends on when you're speaking — so it takes no particle. Nor does "every day", which isn't a point at all.

Parts of the day (あさ, ばん) and しゅうまつ (weekend) normally take no に, but optionally can. Treat them as "no に" and you'll never be wrong, only occasionally less idiomatic.

Frequency adverbs

Three describe how often; two describe how rarely — and the second pair comes with a grammatical string attached:

まいにち · よく · ときどきmainichi · yoku · tokidoki every day · often · sometimes — normal sentence
ぜんぜん · あまりzenzen · amari not at all · not much — require a negative ending
ぜんぜん / あまり … ません these two anticipate the negative, and the sentence must deliver it
わたしは ぜんぜん テレビを みません。…zenzen terebi o mimasen I don't watch TV at all.
たけしさんは あまり べんきょうしません。…amari benkyooshimasen Takeshi doesn't study much.

Saying ×ぜんぜん みます is ungrammatical — the adverb has set up an expectation the verb must satisfy. English has nothing quite like this, which is why it needs deliberate practice rather than just noticing.

Word order

Japanese word order is flexible in the middle and rigid at the end. The verb comes last. Always. Everything before it is a series of noun + particle chunks, and because each chunk carries its own particle, they can be rearranged without confusion:

わたしはtopic
きょうtime
としょかんでplace
にほんごをobject
べんきょうしますverb

"I'll study Japanese at the library today." Move the time chunk to the front and the sentence still means exactly the same thing — because きょう stays きょう wherever it sits. That is what the particles buy you.

Day 5

Inviting, Refusing, and the Kanji

Textbook pp. 90–93, then the kanji list. The social half of the lesson.

〜ませんか — inviting

Take the negative form and add か. That's an invitation:

ひるごはんを たべませんか。hirugohan o tabemasen ka Shall we have lunch? / How about lunch?
テニスを しませんか。tenisu o shimasen ka Would you like to play tennis?
The affirmative does not work this way

たべますか is only ever a question — "do you eat lunch?" It cannot be an invitation. English can invite with either polarity ("shall we eat?" / "won't you eat?"), so this asymmetry has to be learned deliberately. To invite, go negative.

Refusing without saying no

Accepting is easy: いいですね。 Refusing is where the culture sits.

どようびは、ちょっと…doyoobi wa, chotto… Saturday is… a little. — i.e. Saturday doesn't work

ちょっと literally means "a little", and trailing off with it is a standard polite refusal. Japanese speakers don't normally refuse with いいえ — it's too blunt. The unfinished sentence is the politeness: you've signalled the problem without stating it.

Listen for this one. A dropped ちょっと is a "no", and taking it as hesitation you can talk someone out of is a real social mistake.

いく and くる are anchored to the speaker

Someone calls you from another room. In English you shout "I'm coming!" In Japanese you say いきます — "I'm going."

くる = toward where I am  ·  いく = away from where I am anchored to the speaker's position, not the listener's

English anchors "come" to the listener. Japanese anchors both verbs to the speaker. So moving toward the person you're talking to is いく, because you're moving away from yourself.

The topic particle は, properly

You've used は since Lesson 1 as though it marked the subject. It doesn't. The topic need not be the subject at all — it's simply the thing you're announcing you'll talk about:

しゅうまつは たいてい なにを しますか。shuumatsu wa taitee nani o shimasu ka What do you usually do on weekends? — the time is the topic
きょうは きょうとに いきます。kyoo wa Kyooto ni ikimasu Today, I'm going to Kyoto.
ばんごはんは？bangohan wa? How about dinner? — an object made topic, sentence left unfinished

That last one is worth noticing: ばんごはん is what would be eaten — the object — yet it takes は. Read は as "as for…" rather than "the subject is", and these all become predictable.

Workbook and Kanji Practice

Workbook pp. 32–39, plus the kanji sheets at workbook pp. 133–134.

Eight sheets, one per grammar point, ending with 答えましょう (written questions) and 聞く練習 (listening). Production day — everything so far has been recognition, and building a sentence from nothing is a different skill.

If you're running short

Do the odd-numbered sheets and the Using Kanji page, and skip the tracing. Never skip the listening sheet — it is the only thing in this lesson that makes you decode Japanese at someone else's pace.

Day 7

Reading: まいにちのせいかつ

Textbook pp. 304–307. The first real passage of the course.

Until now every Japanese sentence you've read was an example built to demonstrate one point. This is a connected passage about someone's daily life, written to be read rather than to teach — and it uses your new verbs, particles, time expressions and kanji all at once.

Run the five-pass protocol. This is the method behind the whole plan's reading claim, and this is the first lesson where you get to use it properly:

  1. Listen only. Audio playing, eyes following. Don't read aloud, don't look anything up. You're installing a model of how it should sound.
  2. Silent read. Mark every place you stalled. Now look up only those — not the whole passage.
  3. Chorus read. Read aloud along with the audio, keeping up. Where you fall behind is where your decoding is still too slow.
  4. Solo read, timed. Aloud, alone, stopwatch running. Write the time in the margin.
  5. Solo read, timed again. Beat pass 4. Write that down too.

Then close the book and answer the comprehension questions from memory. Keep both times. They're your baseline — watching Lesson 5's pass-5 time come in under Lesson 3's is the clearest evidence of progress this plan produces.

Build your own daily routine

The passage describes someone's day. Now describe yours, using only Lesson 3 grammar:

  1. わたしは じかんに おきます。I get up at ___. Clock time takes に.
  2. ばしょで なにを たべます。I eat ___ at ___. で for place, を for object.
  3. まいにち ばしょに いきます。I go to ___ every day. まいにち takes no に.
  4. よく なにを します。I often do ___.
  5. あまり なにを どうしません。I don't ___ much. あまり demands the negative.
  6. じかんごろ ねます。I go to bed at about ___.

Before Lesson 4

Conjugate verbs in your head, constantly. Dictionary form to ます form, in the queue, on the train. Lesson 4 adds the past tense on top of this machinery, and it only works if the machinery is automatic. A verb you have to stop and think about is a verb you can't use in conversation.

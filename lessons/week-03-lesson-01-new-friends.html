Day 1

Greetings, Numbers, Vocabulary, Dialogue

Input day. No analysis, no grammar. You are getting the sound of the language into your ear and the vocabulary in front of your eyes.

In order, with the book open:

  1. Greetings, pp. 30–34. Listen and repeat aloud. These are fixed phrases — don't try to parse them grammatically, because several of them don't parse by the rules you're about to learn. こんにちは ends in は read wa for a reason you'll meet tomorrow.
  2. Numbers, p. 35. Read them aloud. Then read the Expression Notes on numbers at p. 44 — it lists where the readings change irregularly, which is the part that actually catches people. There's a drill for exactly that below.
  3. Vocabulary, pp. 38–40. Read the whole list once with the audio. Don't try to memorise it. The starred words are the ones in the dialogue — those are the priority. The Additional Vocabulary section (countries, majors, occupations, family) is there for you to pick the handful that describe your life; ignore the rest.
  4. Dialogue, pp. 36–37. Listen twice while reading along. First pass: just follow. Second pass: notice how much you can already decode now that you read both scripts.

Day 2

X は Y です

Textbook p. 41. One pattern, and an enormous amount of what you can say in your first month sits inside it.

〜です。 It is… / I am… / That's a…

です attaches to a noun and makes a complete, polite sentence. That's the whole mechanism. がくせいです is a full sentence meaning "(I) am a student."

Three things English does that Japanese doesn't

Each of these feels like something is missing. Nothing is missing — the language simply doesn't mark it.

がくせいです。gakusee desu (I) am a student. — no word for "I"
がくせいです。gakusee desu …or "a student", or "students". No a/the, no plural.
  1. No subject, most of the time. Japanese drops the subject whenever it's obvious who or what is being talked about. A sentence with no "I" in it is normal, not incomplete — and adding one where it isn't needed sounds oddly insistent.
  2. No articles. There is no a and no the.
  3. No plural marking. がくせい is "student" or "students"; context decides.

When you do need to say what you're talking about

If it isn't clear, name the topic and mark it with は:

X は Y です。 X is Y. / As for X, it is Y.
わたしは がくせいです。watashi wa gakusee desu I am a student.
せんこうは にほんごです。senkoo wa nihongo desu My major is Japanese.
やまださんは せんせいです。Yamada san wa sensee desu Mr./Ms. Yamada is a teacher.
は is read wa here — and you already met this

When は does this topic-marking job it is read wa, not ha. This is the rule from week 3, and it is why こんにちは ends the way it does — there's a fossilised topic particle sitting at the end of the greeting. Inside an ordinary word, は is still ha.

Two words to be careful with

さん is never used about yourself. It goes after someone else's name — given or family — as a neutral title. Saying わたしは ○○さんです about yourself is a genuine mistake, not just stiff.

あなた is not the everyday word for "you." English speakers reach for it constantly and it sounds wrong. Use the person's name plus さん instead — you address someone by name even when speaking directly to them. See p. 45.

Check it

Day 3

Questions

Textbook p. 42. Asking is easier in Japanese than in almost any other language you could have picked.

Take any statement. Put か on the end. It's now a question. No word order changes, no auxiliary verb, no "do you".

がくせいです。gakusee desu (I) am a student.
がくせいですか。gakusee desu ka Are (you) a student?

Japanese doesn't customarily use a question mark — the か already does that job, so a full stop is normal.

The なん family

For questions that need more than yes or no, put a question word where the answer would go. Lesson 1 gives you four, all built on なん:

WordAsksExample answer
なんwhatにほんごです
なんじwhat timeくじです
なんさいhow oldにじゅっさいです
なんねんせいwhat year at schoolにねんせいです
なんばんwhat numberろくです
なん or なに?

Same word, two readings. Use なん directly before です or before a counter like じ (o'clock). Use なに before a particle. So なんですか (what is it?) but なにじん (what nationality). You'll meet the なに cases properly in Lesson 3; for now, なん covers almost everything in this lesson.

Answering

Notice that the answer reuses the question's frame and drops the topic, because by then it's obvious:

せんこうは なんですか。senkoo wa nan desu ka What's your major?
けいざいです。keezai desu Economics. — not "my major is economics"

Check it

Day 4

Noun の Noun, and the workbook

Textbook p. 43, then workbook pp. 16–21.

の glues two nouns together. It's often taught as "possessive, like apostrophe-s", which is true but far too narrow — it does much more than ownership.

Noun₁restriction
の
Noun₂main idea

The second noun is what the thing actually is. The first one narrows it down. Get that direction right and の never confuses you again:

たけしさんの でんわばんごうTakeshi san no denwa bangoo Takeshi's phone number — it's a number
だいがくの せんせいdaigaku no sensee a college professor — it's a teacher
にほんごの がくせいnihongo no gakusee a student of Japanese — it's a student
にほんの だいがくNihon no daigaku a university in Japan — it's a university
The test that catches the error

Ask: what is this thing? たけしさんの でんわばんごう is a phone number, so でんわばんごう goes second. Reversing it — でんわばんごうの たけしさん — would mean a Takeshi belonging to a phone number. English speakers reverse this constantly in week one, because English puts the main idea last too ("Takeshi's phone number") but feels like it leads with the owner.

A の phrase behaves like one noun

Once joined, the whole thing slots anywhere a single noun could go — including as the topic of a sentence:

たけしさんの おかあさんは こうこうの せんせいです。 Takeshi san no okaasan wa kookoo no sensee desu Takeshi's mother is a high school teacher.

Two の phrases, one は, one です — and that is a real, natural Japanese sentence built entirely from three days of grammar.

Check it

Day 5

Retrieval and Production

Closed book first, then build something of your own.

Start with the quizzes above, re-run cold — they reshuffle, so a re-run is a new test and not a replay. Then do the thing this lesson was actually for.

Build your じこしょうかい

A self-introduction. Every line below uses only Lesson 1 grammar, so you can construct this entirely from what you now know. Fill the slots with facts about yourself — look up the words you need in the Additional Vocabulary at pp. 39–40.

  1. はじめまして。How do you do. (Fixed phrase, first meeting only.)
  2. なまえです。I'm ___. Your name. No さん on yourself.
  3. くにじんです。I'm ___ese. Country + じん.
  4. だいがくの がくせいです。I'm a student at ___. Noun の Noun.
  5. せんこうは せんこうです。My major is ___. X は Y です.
  6. すうじねんせいです。I'm a ___-year student. Watch よねんせい for 4.
  7. どうぞ よろしく おねがいします。Pleased to meet you. (Fixed phrase.)

Say it out loud, five times, without reading. Then record yourself on your phone and listen back — you will hear things you cannot feel while speaking, especially beat length on がくせい and だいがく.

Ask the other side of it

You can also now ask each of those questions. Practise both roles — say the question, then the answer, then the question again:

おなまえは なんですか。onamae wa nan desu ka What is your name?
せんこうは なんですか。senkoo wa nan desu ka What is your major?
なんねんせいですか。nannensee desu ka What year are you in?
でんわばんごうは なんばんですか。denwa bangoo wa nanban desu ka What's your phone number?

Lesson 1 reference

Grammar and vocabulary, separated — and kept last on purpose. These are lookup tables you return to, not a study day. Genki prints them as sections for the same reason: you will flip back here far more often than you read it through once.

Grammar

1 · Stating what something is

p. 41

X は Y です X is Y.

  • は marks the topic — what the sentence is about — not the subject. It is read wa, never ha.
  • です is not the verb “to be.” It carries politeness, not meaning, which is why it never changes for person or number.
  • No articles, no plurals, no agreement. がくせいです is “I am a student,” “she is a student” or “they are students” — context decides.
  • Drop the topic once it is obvious. にねんせいです alone is a complete, natural sentence.

2 · Asking

p. 42

X は Y ですか Is X Y?

  • Add か. Nothing else moves — no inversion, no auxiliary, no rising intonation required. Japanese makes questions more cheaply than almost any language you could have picked.
  • The なん family replaces the unknown in place: なんですか what · なんじ what time · なんさい how old · なんねんせい which year.
  • Answer with はい、そうです or いいえ — then the correction.

3 · Joining two nouns

p. 43

A の B B, of the A kind. B is the main thing.

  • The direction test: ask what the thing is. にほんごのせんせい is a teacher, not a language.
  • English reverses this, which is the entire difficulty. “Japanese teacher” puts the main noun last too — but “teacher of Japanese” puts it first, and that is the version your instinct will reach for.
  • They chain freely: だいがくのにほんごのせんせい.

4 · Numbers that change their reading

p. 44
  • 4 becomes よ: よじ (4 o’clock), よねんせい (4th-year) — not し.
  • 7 becomes しち: しちじ. 9 becomes く: くじ.
  • Before さい, 1 / 8 / 10 take a small っ: いっさい, はっさい, じゅっさい.
  • 20 years old is はたち — a word in its own right, not a number plus a counter.

5 · Two social rules that behave like grammar

pp. 44–45
  • さん never goes on your own name. It is honorific, so using it about yourself elevates yourself. Same for せんせい.
  • あなた is not the everyday “you.” Use the person’s name plus さん: ハートさんは カナダじんですか.

What you can do now

  • Introduce yourself: name, nationality, school, major, year.
  • Ask another person all of the same, and understand the answer.
  • Say and understand a time, an age, and a phone number.
  • Join two nouns with の — which quietly multiplies everything above, because any noun in any slot can become a two-noun phrase.

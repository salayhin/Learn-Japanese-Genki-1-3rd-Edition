Reference Grammar summary Vocabulary

Six days, and the last lesson in this plan. You gain description — adjectives in both tenses, both polarities, and in front of nouns — plus how to say what you like, how to propose doing something together, and how to count things. Fourteen more kanji, including the four verbs you will see on every menu and signpost. Textbook pp. 128–145.

What makes this lesson tricky

Not the number of forms. It's that you have spent four lessons treating です as the verb, and it isn't. For an い-adjective, です is only politeness bolted on the end — the い is the part that carries tense. Once you see that, all eight forms fall out of two rules. Miss it, and you will produce ×さむいでした for years, which is the single most recognisable beginner error in Japanese.

Day 1

Vocabulary, Dialogues, and the Split

Textbook pp. 128–131. An input day — but one structural fact has to go in first, or the vocabulary will be learned in a form you can't use.

  1. Vocabulary, pp. 130–131, with the audio. Notice that the book lists the adjectives under two separate headings. That is not a formatting choice — it is the most important thing on the page.
  2. Dialogues, pp. 128–129. There are two. The first is a conversation about a holiday, so it's mostly past-tense description; the second is buying T-shirts, which is where the counting at the end of this lesson comes from.

Two classes, and the class is part of the word

Every Japanese adjective belongs to one of two classes, and they conjugate differently. Dictionaries, including Genki's vocabulary list, write the class into the entry: an い-adjective is listed bare, a な-adjective is listed with a な in brackets.

い-adjectives

  • さむい cold (weather)
  • たかい expensive; tall
  • おもしろい interesting; funny
  • むずかしい difficult
  • いそがしい busy
  • つまらない boring

な-adjectives

  • げんき（な） healthy; energetic
  • しずか（な） quiet
  • にぎやか（な） lively
  • ひま（な） free, not busy
  • きれい（な） beautiful; clean
  • きらい（な） disliked
The spelling test fails, and it fails on common words

きれい and きらい both end in い and are both な-adjectives. The reason is visible in the kanji: 綺麗 is read き・れい, so that い is the tail of a reading, not a grammatical ending. You cannot see this in kana. So the class has to be memorised with the word — exactly the discipline you used for verb groups in Lesson 3, and for exactly the same reason: a small number of look-alike exceptions that will otherwise ambush you in every sentence you build.

Three vocabulary items on these pages carry distinctions English doesn't make, and they are worth pinning down on day 1 rather than discovering later:

WordOnly used forOtherwise you'd say
さむい 寒いCold weatherA cold drink is つめたい
あつい 暑いHot weatherA hot drink is あつい 熱い — same sound, different kanji
いそがしい 忙しいBusy people and busy daysA busy city is にぎやか（な）

やさしい is one word doing two jobs: easy of a problem, kind of a person. Both are common and context separates them without effort.

Day 2

Adjectives, Present Tense

Grammar 1, pp. 132–133. Practice I, pp. 138–139. The affirmative is free; the negative is where the two classes separate.

The affirmative is nothing at all: put です on the end. For a な-adjective, drop the bracketed な first.

さむいです   ·   げんきです It is cold.  ·  She is healthy.

The negative is where you have to know the class:

AffirmativeNegative
い-adjective
さむい		 さむいです さむくないですor さむくありません
な-adjective
げんき（な）		 げんきです げんきじゃないですor げんきじゃありません

Look at the な-adjective row again. You have seen じゃないです before — it is the noun negative from Lesson 2, unchanged. That is the shortcut worth holding on to for the rest of the book:

な-adjectives behave like nouns Everything you know about です after a noun applies to them directly.

Why ×さむいじゃないです is wrong

Because じゃないです is the noun pattern, and さむい is not a noun. An い-adjective negates itself, from the inside, by replacing its own final い:

さむいdictionary
→
さむくないnegative
げんきnoun-like stem
→
げんきじゃないnegative

Both くありません and じゃありません exist alongside these and mean the same thing. They are the conservative forms — more at home in writing and in formal speech. The ないです versions are what you will hear. Use those, recognise the others.

The one irregular adjective

いい (good) changes its first syllable to よ in every form except the dictionary form:

いいですIt is good.
よくないですIt is not good.

Compound adjectives built out of いい inherit the irregularity — かっこいい (good-looking) gives かっこよくないです. But かわいい (cute, which arrives in Lesson 7) only looks as if it contains いい; it doesn't, so it behaves normally: かわいくないです.

Turning the dial

Three degree words, all placed before the adjective:

とても きれいですIt is very beautiful.
すごく おいしいですIt is extremely tasty.
ちょっと あついですIt is a little hot.

And the two from Lesson 3 that force a negative ending now apply to adjectives too: あまり おもしろくないです (not very interesting), ぜんぜん むずかしくなかったです (not difficult at all). If you find yourself writing あまり and then an affirmative, the sentence has gone wrong before you reached the end.

Present negative — speed

Type the negative, either with or without です. Both classes are mixed together on purpose: deciding which rule is the skill, and you don't practise it by doing ten of one kind in a row.

Day 3

Past Tense, and Adjectives in Front of Nouns

Grammar 2 and 3, pp. 133–134. Practice II–III, pp. 140–142.

Past affirmativePast negative
い-adjective
さむい		 さむかったです さむくなかったです
な-adjective
げんき（な）		 げんきでした げんきじゃなかったです

Three of those four cells contain かった, which is not a coincidence. Here is the whole of adjective conjugation, compressed:

Past of an い-adjective:   い → かった And every negative ends in ない, which is い-shaped — so the same rule conjugates it.

Follow that through. さむくない ends in い, so it takes かった like any い-adjective: さむくなかった. げんきじゃない also ends in い, so it does the same: げんきじゃなかった. You are not learning four separate past forms — you are learning one, plus でした for the noun-like affirmative you already had from Lesson 4.

This pays off well beyond this lesson. When verbs get their plain negative in Lesson 8, たべない will conjugate to たべなかった by this identical rule. In Japanese, negation is an adjective.

The two errors to watch for in your own writing

×さむいでした — putting the tense on です. Tense lives in かった; です never moves.

×げんきかったです — applying the い rule to a な-adjective. This is the same error as day 2's, in the other direction, and it means the class didn't stick.

Adjectives in front of nouns

So far every adjective has been the predicate — the thing the sentence asserts. They can also sit in front of a noun and describe it, the way English does with "an interesting film". The rule is one line per class:

おもしろいdictionary form, straight in
→
おもしろいえいがan interesting film
きれい（な）the な comes back
→
きれいなしゃしんa beautiful photo

That is what the bracketed な in the vocabulary list has been for all along. It isn't decoration and it isn't optional — it is the form the adjective takes everywhere except directly before です, which is the one place it drops out. Calling them "な-adjectives" names them after their normal shape, not their exception.

Once an adjective is attached, the whole phrase behaves as a single noun and takes particles exactly as a bare noun would:

きのう、おもしろいえいがを みました。Yesterday I saw an interesting film.
きょうとで きれいなしゃしんを とりました。I took a beautiful photo in Kyoto.
ここは とても にぎやかなまちです。This is a very lively city.

Modifiers are never made polite. ×おもしろいですえいが is wrong: politeness attaches once, at the end of the sentence, to the final predicate. This is worth noticing now because it is the first hint of a much larger fact — Japanese marks politeness at sentence level, not word level.

Day 4

好き, きらい, and 〜ましょう

Grammar 4 and 5, pp. 135–136. Expression notes, p. 137. Practice IV–V, pp. 142–143.

In English, like is a verb and takes an object: I like fish. In Japanese it is an adjective, and adjectives have no objects. So the thing you like is not the object — it's the subject, and it takes が.

X は   Y が   すきです As for X, Y is likeable. → X likes Y.
ロバートさんは にほんごのクラスが すきです。Robert likes his Japanese class.
やましたせんせいは さかなが きらいです。Professor Yamashita dislikes fish.

The が is the error everybody makes, and they make it for a good reason: English has trained you to feel that fish is the object, so を feels right. It isn't. If you translate the pattern literally as "as for X, Y is likeable", the が stops feeling strange — が has marked subjects since Lesson 4's あります sentences.

Turning it up, and turning it off

Japanese intensifies these two with a prefix rather than a degree adverb, and the prefixed forms are far commoner than とても好き — the reverse of English, where "very much" is the ordinary intensifier:

だいすきですto love; to like very much
だいきらいですto hate

And if you want neither, the pattern doubles up on も and closes with a single negative:

すきでも きらいでも ないです I neither like it nor dislike it.

Because 好き is a な-adjective, day 3's rule applies unchanged when it modifies a noun: これは わたしの すきな ほんです — this is my favourite book. And when you contrast two things, は displaces が, which is the contrast marker doing the job it has done since Lesson 1: やさいは すきですが、にくは きらいです.

〜ましょう and 〜ましょうか

Take any verb's ます form and swap the ending. That is the entire rule — it is the same stem you have been using since Lesson 3, doing one more job:

たべますpolite form
→
たべましょうlet's eat
のみますpolite form
→
のみましょうかshall we drink?

You now have three ways to propose something, and they are not interchangeable — they sit at different points in the same conversation:

FormWhere it belongs
〜ませんかOpening. Invites someone who has not yet agreed to anything. Negative-shaped because it leaves them room to decline.
〜ましょうかMiddle. Checks a specific proposal — "shall we?" — once the idea is on the table.
〜ましょうSettling. "Let's." Assumes agreement, so it sounds pushy if you use it before you have any.

A real exchange runs straight down that table: いっしょに えいがに いきませんか → いいですね → じゃあ、ろくじに いきましょう.

Two expressions from p. 137

そうですね agrees with what you just heard. Stretch the final ね into そうですねえ and it becomes a stall — the spoken equivalent of "well, let me see", and an extremely useful thing to have when you need three seconds to assemble a sentence.

そうですか is different: falling intonation means "I see, I didn't know that"; rising intonation means "really? are you sure?". Same four syllables, opposite social effect, and the only thing separating them is your pitch.

Day 5

Counting, and Fourteen Kanji

Grammar 6, p. 136, with the counter table at pp. 380–381. Then the kanji at pp. 312–313 and the workbook kanji sheets.

Japanese doesn't count things with a bare number. It uses a number plus a counter — a classifier chosen to match the kind of thing being counted. English does this occasionally, in "three sheets of paper" or "two head of cattle"; Japanese does it always.

Tシャツを   さんまい   かいました I bought three T-shirts. — item first, quantity after.

Two things to take from that sentence. First, the number word comes after the item, which is the reverse of English. Second, the counter phrase has no particle — を is already attached to Tシャツ. The quantity behaves as an adverb modifying the verb, which is why it can float loose in the sentence rather than sitting glued to the noun.

〜まい (枚) is the counter for flat things: sheets of paper, tickets, plates, shirts, cards. It's a good first one to own, because almost everything you buy or are handed while travelling is flat. Other counters — for people, for books, for long thin objects — arrive in later lessons, and the sound changes at the joins follow the same logic as the さんびゃく / ろっぴゃく shifts you met in Lesson 2. The full table is at pp. 380–381; look at it, don't memorise it.

This set breaks into four groups, and learning it that way is far quicker than learning fourteen separate shapes.

Four you can read off the page. 山 is three peaks. 川 is three lines of flowing water. 田 is a paddy divided by its paths. 女 is a seated figure. These are pictographs, the oldest layer of the writing system, and they are drawings that stopped being drawings.

One character that unlocks a dozen words. 気 — spirit, air, mood — is among the highest-frequency kanji in Japanese. 元気 is origin-spirit, your underlying vitality, hence "healthy, energetic" and the greeting お元気ですか. 天気 is heaven-spirit, the weather. Beyond this lesson it gives you 電気 (electricity), 人気 (popularity) and 気持ち (feeling). Watch the pair てんき and でんき: one voicing mark apart, and the mark is doing all the work.

Four verbs, and the division of labour. 見る・行く・食べる・飲む. Look at what the kana after the kanji is doing: 食べる, 食べます, 食べました — the kanji never changes, the kana carries every conjugation. That split, called okurigana, is why kanji make Japanese easier to read once you know a few hundred: the meaning is a fixed shape your eye catches instantly, and the grammar hangs off the end of it. 食 and 飲 together also give you 食べ物 and 飲み物 — the two headings on any menu.

Names and doors. 山, 川 and 田 are three of the commonest components of Japanese surnames — 山川, 山田, 田中, 小川 — so fourteen strokes buy you a real slice of the name-reading problem. And 女 and 男 are the two characters on every public toilet door in Japan. Spend thirty seconds on those two. Getting it wrong is the kind of mistake you only make once.

One reading that isn't a reading

今日 is きょう — "today". Neither character is taking its normal reading; the sound is attached to the compound, not to its parts. Japanese calls these 熟字訓, and there are a few dozen of them. They cannot be worked out, only learned whole, and they are all extremely common — so learn each one the moment you meet it rather than trying to reason about it.

Day 6

Reading: りょこう

Textbook pp. 312–317. Your third five-pass passage — and the last session before the assessment.

This one is worth the whole lesson on its own, because it isn't textbook prose. It's a social-media post and a handwritten postcard: two formats you will actually meet, written the way people actually write them.

  1. Listen only. Audio on, eyes following, nothing looked up.
  2. Silent read. Mark where you stall; look up only those.
  3. Chorus read. Aloud, along with the audio, keeping up.
  4. Solo read, timed. Write the time down.
  5. Solo read, timed again. Beat pass 4.
Three data points now

Put the Lesson 3, Lesson 4 and Lesson 5 times side by side. The absolute numbers matter less than the shape of the gap between pass 4 and pass 5. If that gap is shrinking from lesson to lesson, your decoding is becoming automatic and re-reading is buying you less because there is less left to buy — which is precisely the result you want. Bring all six numbers to the assessment.

Two things the postcard will teach you

It runs vertically. Characters go down the column, and the columns themselves progress right to left, so a vertical text starts at the top-right corner and a vertical book opens from what you would call the back. Nothing about the language changes — but your eyes have never done this, and the first minute is slow. That is normal and it passes quickly.

The address runs largest to smallest. Postcode, then prefecture, then city, then district, then block and house number, and the person's name last. Exactly the reverse of the Western order — and the same logic as a Japanese date, which is year, then month, then day. Once you notice that Japanese consistently goes outside-in, addresses stop needing to be memorised.

Grammar

1 · Two adjective classes

p. 132
  • い-adjectives end in い and conjugate themselves. な-adjectives behave like nouns and are listed with a bracketed な.
  • The spelling test fails. きれい and きらい end in い and are な-adjectives — that い is the tail of a reading (綺麗 = き・れい), not a grammatical ending.
  • So the class is a fact about the word, memorised with it, exactly as with verb groups in Lesson 3.

2 · Present tense

pp. 132–133

さむくないです  ·  げんきじゃないです

  • Affirmative is free: add です.
  • い-adjective negative: drop い, add くないです.
  • な-adjective negative: じゃないです — Lesson 2’s noun pattern, completely unchanged.
  • ×さむいじゃないです is the commonest error in the lesson: the noun pattern applied to an い-adjective.
  • いい is the one irregular — よくないです. かっこいい inherits it; かわいい does not, because it isn’t built from いい.
  • とても, すごく, ちょっと go before the adjective.

3 · Past tense — and the rule that unifies it

pp. 133–134

さむかったです  ·  げんきでした

  • い-adjectives: い → かった. な-adjectives: でした, because they are noun-like.
  • Every negative ends in ない, which is い-shaped — so the same rule conjugates it: さむくない→さむくなかった, げんきじゃない→げんきじゃなかった. Eight forms collapse into three facts.
  • Watch ×さむいでした (tense on です, which never carries it) and ×げんきかったです (the い rule on the wrong class).

4 · Adjectives in front of nouns

p. 134

おもしろい えいが  ·  きれいな しゃしん

  • い-adjectives go in as their plain dictionary form, no particle, no です.
  • The bracketed な returns in front of a noun. It only disappears directly before です — which is the exception, not the rule.
  • Modifiers are never made polite: ×おもしろいですえいが. Politeness attaches once, at the end of the sentence.

5 · 好き and きらい

p. 135

X は Y が すきです

  • 好き is an adjective, not a verb, so it has no object. The thing you like is the subject and takes が.
  • を feels right only because English makes “like” a verb. Read it as “as for X, Y is likeable” and the が stops being strange.
  • だいすき to love · だいきらい to hate — both commoner than adding とても.
  • Neither one: すきでもきらいでもないです.

6 · 〜ましょう and 〜ましょうか

p. 136

たべましょう

  • Take the ます form and swap the ending. Same stem you have used since Lesson 3.
  • ましょう settles it (“let’s”) · ましょうか checks (“shall we?”) · Lesson 3’s ませんか invites someone who has not yet agreed.
  • A real exchange runs straight down that list: ませんか → いいですね → ましょう.

7 · Counting things

p. 136

Ｔシャツを さんまい かいました

  • A number plus a counter chosen to match the kind of thing. 〜まい is for flat things — tickets, plates, shirts, paper.
  • The quantity comes after the item, the reverse of English.
  • The counter phrase takes no particle — it behaves as an adverb modifying the verb, which is why it floats loose. Full counter table at pp. 380–381.

What you can do now

  • Describe anything — in the present or the past, positive or negative.
  • Put a description in front of a noun, which is how most real sentences carry them.
  • Say what you like, love, dislike and hate, and ask someone else.
  • Propose doing something together, at the right level of pushiness.
  • Count flat objects, and read a price, a date and a day of the week.

If you carry on

Lesson 6 is the て-form, and it is the highest-leverage single thing in elementary Japanese. It is the hinge that connects clauses, makes requests, expresses permission, and builds the progressive — most of what you currently cannot say is waiting behind it. Everything in this lesson feeds it: the て-form has its own conjugation table, and you will learn it far faster because you have now done this twice, once for verbs and once for adjectives, and know what the exercise feels like.
